## 1. Solution overview

The deliverable is an **exec communication story outline** the AI Enablement Lead uses when they have to talk about their own work to an executive audience whose decisions control the Lead's scope, authority, budget, and trajectory. The audience is senior sponsors, peer heads-of, the governance council, and — one layer up — the board or board committee that reads the scorecard.

The outline is **not** the weekly scorecard, the quarterly board review, or the internal roadmap. Those answer "what is the state of the program?" This outline answers a different question: "what has this program (and by extension, this Lead) done, where does it go next, and what does the exec need to decide or back?" It is the story that frames the Lead's operating artifacts for people whose attention is measured in minutes, not pages.

Posture checks this solution encodes:

- **One story, many venues.** The same arc gets retold at a 1:1 skip-level, a quarterly review, a town hall, a promotion packet, a board committee read-out, and an external interview. The venue changes voice and length; the arc does not.
- **Program outcome first, Lead leadership second.** The AI Enablement Lead's career case is built *on* the program's outcomes, not instead of them. Starting with "what I built" instead of "what the business got" is a reliable signal of someone who has not yet earned the next rung.
- **Attribution is honest.** Collective CoE work is named as collective; the Lead's contribution is specific. Mis-attribution is a reference-check failure waiting to happen.
- **The ask is named.** Every exec story has one of three asks — a decision, a resource, or backing. A story without an ask is a status update dressed as a story; executives will treat it accordingly.
- **The story survives a hostile read.** It is written so a skeptical reader (incoming CFO, newly-appointed audit chair, a regulator's liaison) comes away with the same bounded, defensible view as a friendly reader.
- **Confidentiality holds.** Specific metrics, vendors, incidents, and names are used only where the Lead has standing to share them in that venue. The outline marks what is in-scope per venue.
- **The story carries a next-step ambition subtly, not loudly.** The outline does not read as a job-hunt pitch; it reads as an operator speaking plainly about the program, with the next step legible as scope expansion rather than exit.
- **Posture is grounded in frameworks the reader already uses** — NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 if relevant, sector-regulatory. The exec reader anchors faster when the story cites a frame they recognise.

## 2. Implementation — worked answer (`exec-communication-story-outline.md`)

### 2.1 Scope and audience catalog

The outline is calibrated for six recurring exec venues. Voice, length, and the specific ask differ by venue; the underlying arc is constant.

| Venue | Reader | Length | Dominant ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| **V1. Skip-level 1:1** (exec sponsor's manager, or a peer head-of) | 1 person, high context | 10-15 min spoken | Backing on a specific thing |
| **V2. Governance council quarterly** | 6-12 cross-function heads | 20 min spoken + pre-read | Decision on policy / scope change |
| **V3. Board risk & technology committee** | 3-7 board members | 8 min spoken + 3-page memo | Backing or informational posture |
| **V4. All-hands / town hall** | 100-5000+ employees | 5-7 min spoken | Legitimacy signal + call to action |
| **V5. Promotion packet / scope review** | Promotion committee, HR partner | 1-page narrative + artifacts | Decision on the Lead's promotion or scope |
| **V6. External interview / recruiter screen** | 1 hiring manager or recruiter | 10 min spoken | Fit assessment |

The outline below is the **master arc**. Sections 2.4 and 2.5 show how the same arc is compressed or expanded per venue.

### 2.2 The five-beat master arc

Every retelling of the story hits these five beats, in this order. Executives read narratively — out-of-order beats break their pattern-match and trigger status-update mode.

```
Beat 1 — Where we were           (the condition that justified the role)
Beat 2 — What we did              (the program actions, named as collective where collective)
Beat 3 — What the business got    (outcomes against plan, with attribution-honesty bound)
Beat 4 — Where we go next         (the next-quarter / next-year direction)
Beat 5 — What I am asking of you  (the decision / resource / backing)
```

Each beat has a canonical shape and a fail-state.

#### Beat 1 — Where we were (the condition)

- **Shape:** 1-3 sentences that name the condition the program was created to change. "N months ago, tool adoption was ad-hoc, governance was absent, and the single biggest risk was [specific pattern]." The condition is named in the vocabulary the exec reader already uses (regulatory, financial, talent, operational).
- **Fail-state:** opening with the Lead's role or title ("As the AI Enablement Lead, I…"). The role is not the condition; the condition is why the role exists.

#### Beat 2 — What we did (the actions)

- **Shape:** 3-5 named actions at the right altitude. For a quarterly venue: "stood up the governance council, ratified the acceptable-use policy, consolidated from N vendors to M, trained K% of eligible staff to the role-tier floor." For a town hall: narrative version. The actions are named **as collective where they are collective**; "we" is the default pronoun. The Lead's specific contribution is callable out in Q&A but not front-loaded.
- **Fail-state:** list-of-activities syndrome — ten bullets, five of which are things any role would have done. The actions stated are the ones that moved an outcome in Beat 3.

#### Beat 3 — What the business got (outcomes with attribution)

- **Shape:** the outcomes from the quarterly scorecard restated in the exec's vocabulary, **with an explicit attribution bound**. "Adoption is on plan; sustained usage is trending up and is the leading indicator we watch. We do not yet claim productivity causation beyond what the ROI defence memo supports." Non-regression floors (safety, cost, compliance, talent sentiment) are named as held or at-risk.
- **Fail-state:** unbounded ROI claims ("we saved $X"), causal claims the data does not support, or no outcome section at all (activities-only).

#### Beat 4 — Where we go next (direction)

- **Shape:** the next planning horizon's 2-3 priorities in one paragraph. The priorities tie back to the condition in Beat 1 (progress against the original problem) and the gap left in Beat 3 (what is not yet claimable). A horizon length is named (next quarter, next half, next year).
- **Fail-state:** the roadmap page from a slide deck. Executives do not need the backlog; they need the shape of the next horizon.

#### Beat 5 — What I am asking of you (the ask)

- **Shape:** one of three — a **decision** (ratify a policy, approve a budget, approve a scope change), a **resource** (headcount, tool budget, named exec time), or **backing** (publicly named sponsorship of a hard call). The ask is specific, bounded in time, and reversible-or-not is stated. If there is no ask, the story is tagged *informational — no ask this cycle* in the opening, so the reader does not spend the whole read waiting for one.
- **Fail-state:** no ask, or multiple asks diffused across the story. One ask per telling.

### 2.3 The quarterly worked example (archetype org)

An illustrative retelling of the master arc for a 3,000-person regulated enterprise at the end of its fourth quarter under a standing AI Enablement Lead. Specifics are illustrative, not operational; the shape is the point.

> **Beat 1 — Where we were.** Four quarters ago, the organisation ran eleven AI tools across BUs with no central acceptable-use policy and no shared measurement. The audit function flagged AI as the single largest uncontrolled technology exposure in that year's internal audit. Our regulatory counterpart asked us when we expected to meet their operator-literacy expectations. The CoE was commissioned in response.
>
> **Beat 2 — What we did.** Over four quarters, we ratified the acceptable-use policy with governance, consolidated the tool stack from eleven to six with one remaining retirement scheduled, stood up the champion network at twenty-three named champions across all major BUs, and ran the role-tier literacy curriculum to a point where we are above the mod-104 floor for every role tier with a disclosure-eligible operator footprint. <!-- org-specific: replace scope, counts, and jurisdictional specifics with the organisation's actuals -->
>
> **Beat 3 — What the business got.** Measured against the mod-106 scorecard, adoption is on plan and sustained usage is now our leading indicator (`exercise-02`-style scorecard view available on request). Three incidents required rollback this year under the mod-109 posture; all three have recurrence controls now in place and none escalated to regulatory reporting. On ROI, the mod-107 defence memo supports a bounded productivity-upside claim for two of six workstreams; we do not yet claim program-level ROI causation beyond that bound. The non-regression floors — safety, cost, compliance, talent sentiment — held all four quarters.
>
> **Beat 4 — Where we go next.** Next horizon has three priorities: finish the tool consolidation (one remaining retirement), close the two Article-equivalent literacy-floor gaps in the sector-specific role tiers, and commission one internal agent under the mod-110 posture to prove the commissioning standard holds at scale. We will not expand the tool stack into a seventh platform this horizon.
>
> **Beat 5 — What I am asking of you.** I am asking the committee to ratify the updated acceptable-use policy appendix covering the internal-agent commissioning posture (mod-110 standard; two-page redline circulated with the pre-read). The ratification is scope-conditional on the pre-read; the policy, once ratified, is in effect for twelve months before next review.

The retelling is ~350 words and ~2 spoken minutes. Governance-council venues get this with 2-3 slides; the skip-level venue gets a compressed version (Beat 1+3+5 only); the town hall gets a Beat-2-heavy version with a specific call to action.

### 2.4 Venue compression / expansion rules

The master arc stays. What changes per venue:

| Venue | Compression | What else |
|---|---|---|
| **V1. Skip-level 1:1** | Beats 1, 3, 5 only. Beats 2 and 4 are available on-ask in Q&A. | High-bandwidth; the Lead can talk through the attribution bound in paragraph form. |
| **V2. Governance council quarterly** | All five beats. Beat 2 is 3-5 bullets; Beat 4 names the two or three priorities for next horizon. | Pre-read includes the mod-106 scorecard and open-exception list; the spoken version does not re-read these. |
| **V3. Board risk & technology committee** | All five beats, in prose — not bullets. Beat 3 is the longest; attribution-honesty paragraph is ≥ 1/3 of Beat 3. | The named material incidents from the quarter are in a separate section of the memo; the spoken version references them but does not re-read them. The ask is one sentence. |
| **V4. All-hands / town hall** | Beats 1, 2, 4. Beat 3 is a single line ("we measure the following outcomes and publish the scorecard quarterly"); Beat 5 is a call to action, not a decision. | Voice is populated, not quantified. No scorecard numbers unless publicly disclosed. |
| **V5. Promotion packet / scope review** | All five beats, written. Beat 2 names the Lead's specific contributions alongside the collective "we"; this is the only venue where personal attribution is front-loaded. | Beat 5's ask is the promotion / scope decision itself. The packet carries the artifact portfolio (mod-111 exercise-05 Family D). |
| **V6. External interview / recruiter screen** | All five beats, voice-first. Beat 2 drops organisation-specific detail (named tools, named vendors, named incidents) and keeps pattern. Beat 3 keeps the attribution bound but drops specific scorecard numbers unless publicly disclosed. | Beat 5 is reframed — not "what I ask of you" but "what I am looking for in the next role." |

### 2.5 Voice and register

- **Default voice:** first-person plural ("we"), past tense for actions, present tense for posture, conditional for direction.
- **First-person singular ("I")** only in Beat 5 (the ask), in V5 (promotion packet) Beat 2, and in V6 (external interview). Over-use of "I" in Beats 1-4 is the single most reliable tell of an operator who is pitching themselves at the expense of the program.
- **No weasels.** "Significant," "substantial," "material," "approximately" — these are not language the board remembers. Specific numbers or specific words. Beat 3's attribution bound is explicit: "the evidence supports X; it does not yet support Y."
- **No internal jargon.** "Commissioning brief," "governance ritual," "CoP field report" — these are internal terms. Outside V1-V2, use "formal intake," "quarterly review," "practitioner notes" or equivalent.
- **No surprise numbers.** Any number in Beats 3 or 4 has already been seen by the exec sponsor in the mod-106 scorecard or the mod-107 ROI memo. The exec reader should recognise the number, not be introduced to it.
- **No criticism of other functions.** If a non-CoE team is named as having not moved something, the frame is collaborative, not accusatory.

### 2.6 Confidentiality posture per venue

What is in-scope per venue. Anything not in-scope is held or paraphrased.

| Venue | Internal metrics | Named vendors | Named incidents | Named individuals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| V1. Skip-level | Full scorecard | Yes, in context | Yes, in context | Yes, as collaborators |
| V2. Governance council | Full scorecard | Yes, named reviews | Yes, with recurrence status | Yes, as collaborators |
| V3. Board committee | Pre-read + bounded spoken references | Patterns, not named | Named materially, with recurrence status | Named role, not name |
| V4. Town hall | Only publicly-published scorecard items | No | Only publicly-disclosed | Role / team names only |
| V5. Promotion packet | Full scorecard with sign-off from exec sponsor | Yes, named, with sign-off | Yes, named, with sign-off | Collaborators named with their consent |
| V6. External interview | Patterns and public-published numbers only | No | No specifics beyond public disclosure | No named individuals |

The two-part confidentiality test from mod-111 exercise-05 applies: *could this be published if my employer's name were attached?* and *would I be comfortable if my exec sponsor read the piece before I delivered it?* Any content that fails either is paraphrased or held.

### 2.7 Pre-read contract and meeting economics

For V2 and V3, the story is a spoken 5-20 minutes; the pre-read is where the data lives. The pre-read contract:

- **Delivered ≥ 3 business days before the meeting** so the reader has time.
- **≤ 3 pages for V3; ≤ 6 pages for V2.** Longer pre-reads do not get read.
- **Contains:** the scorecard snapshot (mod-106 exercise-02), the open-exceptions and open-high-severity list, the attribution-bound statement (mod-106 exercise-06), the specific ask with any accompanying redline (for V2) or memo (for V3).
- **Does not contain:** the backlog, the vendor-review working documents, the champion-CoP field reports, or anything from mod-109 incident deep-dives beyond named incidents with status.

The spoken version is for the five beats and the discussion. If the meeting drifts into reading the pre-read aloud, the pre-read was too long or delivered too late.

### 2.9 Decision rationale — why this shape, not another

- **Why five beats, in this order.** Executives pattern-match narratively: condition → actions → outcomes → direction → ask. Out-of-order tellings (starting with the ask, or starting with activities) break the pattern-match and the reader spends attention on reordering instead of on the decision.
- **Why "we" is the default, not "I".** The CoE's outcomes are collective. An operator who says "I ratified the policy" when six people co-authored it reads as unreliable in exec circles that cross-reference. The one venue where "I" leads (V5 promotion packet) is the venue where personal attribution is the question on the table; everywhere else, "we" is both correct and strategically sound.
- **Why one ask per telling.** Multiple asks compete for the same decision-making budget and none get decided cleanly. One ask, named, time-bounded, is what moves.
- **Why the attribution bound is explicit in Beat 3.** Executives read ROI claims with a prior of skepticism, especially in regulated sectors. Naming what the evidence supports *and* what it does not increases trust more than any specific number does.
- **Why the pre-read is capped at 3 or 6 pages.** Longer pre-reads do not get read. A pre-read that gets read is more useful than a comprehensive pre-read that is skimmed.
- **Why V3 (board) is prose, not bullets.** Board readers read packets; bullets without prose read as evasive. Prose with specific numbers reads as grounded.
- **Why V6 (external) drops organisation-specific detail.** The confidentiality test fails for most specifics; the pattern-level telling still demonstrates competence and respects employer loyalty. Hiring managers who are good at hiring hear the pattern and judge the operator; hiring managers who are not can be filtered.
- **Why V4 (town hall) uses no scorecard numbers unless publicly disclosed.** Internal numbers announced in a town hall travel outside the room and are not controllable. Town-hall voice is populated, not quantified.
- **Why the ask in V5 (promotion) is the promotion itself, not a program decision.** V5 is the one venue where the Lead's career is the thing on the table. Conflating it with a program ask weakens both.
- **Why the master arc stays constant across venues.** A Lead who has internalised one arc can retell it fluently under cold-call conditions (hallway, elevator, surprise board question). A Lead juggling six different arcs stumbles under pressure, and that stumble is read as un-ready for the next rung.

## 3. Validation steps

1. **Six venues cataloged** (V1 skip-level, V2 governance council, V3 board committee, V4 town hall, V5 promotion packet, V6 external) with reader, length, and dominant ask.
2. **Five-beat master arc defined** with canonical shape and fail-state per beat, in a fixed order.
3. **Per-venue compression rules** named for which beats are included and how the voice shifts.
4. **Worked example** at the archetype organisation present, covering all five beats, with `<!-- org-specific: ... -->` marks on any numeric specifics.
5. **Voice and register guide** names first-person-plural default, "I" exceptions, no-weasels rule, no-internal-jargon rule, no-surprise-numbers rule.
6. **Confidentiality posture matrix** names what is in-scope per venue across metrics, vendors, incidents, and individuals.
7. **Pre-read contract** for V2 and V3 names delivery timing, length cap, included-doc list, excluded-doc list.
8. **Attribution-honesty paragraph required in Beat 3** for V2, V3, V5 venues.
9. **One-ask discipline** named and enforced across venues.
10. **Reusable story-outline template** provided as copy-pasteable markdown.
11. **Decision rationale** names why this arc, this order, this per-venue compression, this confidentiality posture.
12. **`org-specific` tags** on all organisation-dependent specifics (scope numbers, incident counts, policy-redline specifics, jurisdictional literacy-floor language).

## 5. Common mistakes

- **Opening with the role instead of the condition.** "As the AI Enablement Lead, I have driven…" — the role is not the story; the condition the role exists to change is the story. Open with the condition.
- **"I" instead of "we" in Beats 1-4.** The CoE's work is collective. First-person-singular in beats where the work is collective is a reliable reference-check failure trigger.
- **Unbounded ROI claims in Beat 3.** "We saved $X" without the attribution bound is a claim executives mentally tax before giving credit; the net signal is often negative.
- **Activities-only storytelling.** Beat 2 (what we did) without Beat 3 (what the business got) is a status update. Executives read status updates in the pre-read, not in a spoken story.
- **No ask, or multiple asks.** A story with no named ask drifts; a story with three competing asks lands none. One ask, specific, time-bounded.
- **Reading the pre-read aloud.** The pre-read is the data; the spoken version is the arc. If the meeting becomes a pre-read reading, the pre-read was too long or delivered too late.
- **Internal jargon at V3 / V4 / V6.** "Commissioning brief" means nothing outside the CoE context; "formal intake" is the external translation.
- **Surprise numbers in Beat 3.** A scorecard number the exec reader has not seen before triggers skepticism and spawns side-questions. Numbers that appear in Beat 3 are numbers already in the mod-106 scorecard circulation.
- **V4 (town hall) with internal scorecard numbers.** Numbers announced to 5,000 employees travel outside the room. Keep town-hall voice populated, not quantified.
- **V6 (external interview) with named vendors or named incidents.** Fails the confidentiality test; signals the Lead will do the same in a future employer's reference check.
- **V5 (promotion packet) that loads Beat 3 with program ROI and skips the Lead's specific contribution.** The one venue where personal attribution is the question on the table, and the one venue where "I" is appropriate in Beat 2.
- **No attribution-honesty paragraph in Beat 3.** The paragraph is what separates an operator who reads as reliable from one who reads as pitching.
- **Criticism of other functions.** Any sentence that names another function as a blocker in Beat 2 or Beat 4 is a sentence that will be relayed. Keep the frame collaborative.
- **Pre-read delivered the morning of the meeting.** Reads as disorganised; the meeting drifts into reading. Three business days, minimum.

### 5.1 Stretch — the hostile-read drill

Before any V2 or V3 delivery, the Lead runs the outline through a hostile-read drill. A trusted peer or mentor reads it as if they were:

- An incoming CFO who has never seen the program, whose prior is cost-skepticism.
- An audit chair whose prior is control-skepticism.
- A regulator liaison whose prior is scope-skepticism.
- A board member one quarter from a public AI incident at another company, whose prior is risk-aversion.

Each reader-posture generates one or two edits. Patterns that recur across reader-postures (unbounded claims, missing attribution, missing recurrence status on named incidents) get fixed before delivery. The drill is a 30-minute exercise; it catches more than any solo re-read.
